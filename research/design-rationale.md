# Why the Course Looks Like This

> This page explains the main design decisions in Vibe Coding 101 and the evidence behind each one. It's written for instructors adapting the course, for reviewers, and for students who want to know why they're being asked to do things this way.
> The evidence comes from the course's research review, completed in September 2026: the full [landscape report](landscape-report-2026-09.md) and the detailed [research notes](notes/).

## The short version

**Teach the loop, not the tool.** University and industry courses on AI-assisted programming have converged on one shape: vibe-code and deploy something in week one, climb from chat to editor to agent, add specs, git, tests, review and security along the way, and grade students on explaining their code rather than on whether it runs ([report](landscape-report-2026-09.md)). The research supports that shape. AI help raises task completion, but without structure it weakens debugging and code comprehension, and struggling students come away believing they understand more than they do ([Prather et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3632620.3671116); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); [Bastani et al.](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). Vibe-coded apps fail in predictable places: public database keys without access rules, leaked secrets, invented package names and destructive agent commands ([report](landscape-report-2026-09.md#beginner-apps-break-where-browser-meets-server-not-in-the-syntax)). And the free tools change monthly.

So the course teaches one tool-agnostic workflow, [the Safe Loop](../resources/safe-loop.md); gives every tool two tested fallbacks; saves everything to GitHub; checks understanding hardest in the agent weeks; builds its security unit around real breaches; and keeps every product detail in one dated file, [TOOLS.md](../TOOLS.md).

## How strong is the evidence?

Not all of it is equally strong, and the course is designed with that in mind.

- **Randomized trials** exist for a few questions: how AI help affects learning ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); [Bastani et al.](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), which studied high-school *math*, not coding) and how it affects experienced developers' speed ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)).
- **Observational studies and surveys** describe what novices actually do when vibe coding ([Geng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22614); [Fawzy et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24521v1)) and how courses are assessed ([Geng et al. syllabus study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05898); [ACM Task Force](https://acm-education-genai-task-force.github.io/ACM_Taskforce_GenAI_Report_16Feb26.pdf)).
- **Course reports** such as CMU 15-113's are rich but self-reported, and its instructor notes that an LLM drafted most of the report before he revised it ([Student Wisdom report](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~113/bestPractices.html)).
- **Practitioner consensus** (vendor guides and well-known engineers) supports the workflow itself. It is strong agreement, not controlled evidence.
- **Security figures** come mostly from vendors with a commercial interest ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/blog/spring-2026-genai-code-security/); [GitGuardian](https://blog.gitguardian.com/the-state-of-secrets-sprawl-2026/)), though they point the same way.

Where a decision rests on weaker evidence, the tables below say so.

## Decisions and evidence

### Sequence and pacing

| Decision | Evidence | Where it appears |

| **Start with vibe coding in week 1**, and deploy something that day | Across university and industry courses, students vibe-code and deploy a prototype in week one ([report](landscape-report-2026-09.md)). | **A ladder of tools**: chat with preview → app builders → editor assistant → own server → agent | CMU 15-113 put its first project on browser chatbots so students would feel that workflow's limits before meeting an editor assistant ([Student Wisdom report](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~113/bestPractices.html)). Northeastern's CS 7180 climbs the same ladder explicitly ([NEU CS 7180](https://johnguerra.co/classes/aiCoding_spring_2026/)). Each rung comes after the habits it needs. | [Syllabus §4](../SYLLABUS.md#the-ladder-of-tools) |
| **Spec first, in week 3**, with WHEN … THE APP SHALL … criteria | Vendor guides agree on spec or plan before code ([GitHub Spec Kit](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/spec-driven-development-with-ai-get-started-with-a-new-open-source-toolkit/); [Codex best practices](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices); [Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). EARS-style criteria are learnable by non-programmers ([Kiro](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/feature-specs/)). | **Read, debug and own the code in week 4**, with an AI-off Debug Clinic and "the AI plans, you write, the AI reviews" | Debugging is the skill AI use erodes most ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). | **Deploy early and often**: GitHub Pages in week 1, a dedicated deployment and secrets lab in week 5 | CMU students reported that deployment took more time than coding ([Student Wisdom report](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~113/bestPractices.html)). | **Hold agents until week 6** | UChicago and Northeastern both reserve autonomous agents for the second half ([UChicago](https://mpcs-courses.cs.uchicago.edu/2025-26/spring/courses/mpcs-51238-1); [NEU](https://johnguerra.co/classes/aiCoding_spring_2026/)). DORA finds AI amplifies existing practice, and names strong version control and small batches as conditions for it to help ([DORA](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_dora_ai_capabilities_model.pdf)), so git, specs and tests come first. At CMU, understanding and code-reading scores fell once agents took over ([Student Wisdom report](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~113/bestPractices.html)). | [Week 6](../weeks/06-agents/) |
| **Open the agent week by "being the agent"**: change an app using only an agent's tools | Utah had students change a calculator using only an agent's tools ([Utah calculator repo](https://github.com/utah-cs3960-sp26/calculator)). Stanford's Fall 2026 course opens by showing students how an agent works inside ([CS146S](https://themodernsoftware.dev/)). Both show the loop at no cost before students hand it control. | [Be the Agent](../weeks/06-agents/be-the-agent/) |
| **Security and review in week 7**, built around an attack-your-own-database lab | The most instructive failures share one root cause: a public key plus missing authorization rules ([Superblocks on Lovable](https://www.superblocks.com/blog/lovable-vulnerabilities); [Wiz on Moltbook](https://www.wiz.io/blog/exposed-moltbook-database-reveals-millions-of-api-keys)). | **Four projects, from toy to real stakes**: home page → game → a tool you'd use → deployed capstone | The near-universal opening is a single-file page, then a game, which needs no backend and gives instant feedback ([DLAI](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/build-with-andrew); [Google codelab](https://codelabs.developers.google.com/vibe-code-with-gemini-in-aistudio)). Vanderbilt builds on learners' own tools and files ([Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/build-anything-with-ai)). ## What we deliberately did not do

**A course built around one vendor.** Stanford's Fall 2025 course featured one partner tool per week. Its week 2 relied on a free year of Cursor Pro that has since closed to new sign-ups, so that week can no longer be reproduced as written ([report](landscape-report-2026-09.md#existing-courses-start-with-vibe-coding-then-hand-students-more-autonomous-tools)). Vendor academies are free but tied to one tool, and some need a paid plan ([Anthropic Academy](https://anthropic.skilljar.com/claude-code-101)). This course names products only where a step needs them and keeps the specifics in [TOOLS.md](../TOOLS.md).

**AI detectors.** The course *requires* AI, so the question is never "did you use it?" but "can you explain what you shipped?" Educators are moving away from trying to make assignments AI-proof ([ACM Task Force](https://acm-education-genai-task-force.github.io/ACM_Taskforce_GenAI_Report_16Feb26.pdf)). Effort-graded labs, `PROMPTS.md` logs, AI-free writing and oral walkthroughs make detection unnecessary.

**Required paid tools.** The report found that if a course *must* require one paid tier, the evidence supports a cheap editor-assistant upgrade or one month of a frontier agent timed to weeks 6–8. We made both optional ([Syllabus §10](../SYLLABUS.md#10-tools-and-cost)), because the free path works, and learners criticize courses that require paid subscriptions ([Coursera reviews](https://www.coursera.org/learn/claude-code/reviews)).

**Agents in week 1.** Agents amplify whatever habits a student already has ([DORA](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)), and understanding dropped when agents took over at CMU ([Student Wisdom report](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~113/bestPractices.html)). The destructive-agent incidents in the [case studies](../resources/case-studies.md) show what an agent does without git, tests and containment. We borrowed the useful part of early agent teaching, showing how an agent works from the inside, but placed it in week 6 as [Be the Agent](../weeks/06-agents/be-the-agent/), with no AI at all.

**Cursor or Gemini CLI as the primary tools.** Both were strong choices in 2025. By mid-2026, Cursor's free plan lacked MCP and its free student year had closed, and Gemini CLI's free Google login had ended, while its own README still advertised the old quota for months ([report](landscape-report-2026-09.md#free-tiers-still-exist-in-2026-but-they-are-capped-age-restricted-and-change-monthly); dates in [TOOLS.md](../TOOLS.md)). Copilot Free kept agent mode, MCP and a CLI at no cost, with no age restriction.

**Banning outside AI in favor of a course tutor only.** CS50 allows only its own tutor ([CS50](https://cs50.harvard.edu/x/2026/honesty/)). That suits a course whose goal is learning to program by hand. This course's goal is learning to direct AI tools safely, so AI is required, and the hint-only tutor is used only where the goal is the student's own thinking: the Debug Clinic.

**Grading on whether the app runs.** A working demo is required for the capstone but earns little on its own. Robustness, specs, tests, security and explanation carry the grade, following [Utah](https://github.com/utah-cs3960-sp26/syllabus/blob/main/syllabus.md) and [Michigan](https://eecs498-aase.github.io/syllabus.html).

## Open questions and how this course measures itself

### What nobody knows yet

The report is explicit about the gaps ([report](landscape-report-2026-09.md#ai-raises-output-but-lowers-understanding-unless-teachers-add-structure); [conclusion](landscape-report-2026-09.md#conclusion)):

- **No controlled study has compared course orderings.** The shared shape of existing courses rests on design rationale and student surveys ([Geng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05898)).
- **No semester-long randomized trial has tested teaching non-programmers through vibe coding.** Studies of novices vibe coding are small (19 students in [Geng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22614)) or surveys.
- **No study has tested whether writing specs or tests first helps novices learn.** The case is practitioner consensus.
- **No study shows how much programming knowledge non-programmers need to vibe-code safely.** The [six skills](../resources/without-ai-skills.md) are an expert-consensus floor, not a measured one.
- **The best evidence for hint-only tutors is from high-school math** ([Bastani et al.](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). The course tutor is an untested adaptation.
- **Productivity evidence is contested.** METR's 2025 and 2026 studies point in different directions, and METR calls its 2026 estimates unreliable ([METR 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)).

### How the course checks itself

Because of those gaps, the course measures its own students' understanding before and after, as the report recommends.

| Measure | What it tells you | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre/post self-assessment** against the eight learning outcomes | How students' confidence changes over the course | [assessment/self-assessment.md](../assessment/self-assessment.md) |
| **Exit tickets**, weekly and AI-free | Which of the six skills are shaky, week by week | [assessment/exit-tickets.md](../assessment/exit-tickets.md) |
| **Oral walkthrough results** in weeks 4, 6 and 8 | Whether understanding holds up once agents arrive: the CMU warning sign | [assessment/oral-walkthroughs.md](../assessment/oral-walkthroughs.md) |
| **Confidence vs explanation** in each reflection: rate your confidence, then explain the part you're least sure of | Calibration: does felt understanding match demonstrated understanding? | [REFLECTION template](../templates/REFLECTION.md) |
| **A short survey after each project**, as CMU 15-113 ran ([Student Wisdom report](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~113/bestPractices.html)) | Tool problems, workload, perceived understanding and code reading, early enough to act | Instructor's choice of form |
| **A log of tool failures and fallback switches** | Whether the fallbacks in TOOLS.md actually work for a cohort | [instructor/pre-cohort-checklist.md](../instructor/pre-cohort-checklist.md) |

If you teach the course, compare self-assessment with walkthrough results. A gap between the two, especially in weeks 6–8, is the signal the research predicts. Please share what you find through an issue or discussion ([CONTRIBUTING](../CONTRIBUTING.md)).

## How to update the course

1. **Tool changes go in [TOOLS.md](../TOOLS.md) only.** That is the point of the [two-layer rule](../CONTRIBUTING.md#the-two-layer-rule). If a concept page needs changing because a tool changed, the page probably named something it shouldn't have.
2. **Before each cohort, and again at mid-course, actually use every primary and fallback tool** instead of trusting the docs; Google's own pages lagged its changes by months. The steps are in [instructor/pre-cohort-checklist.md](../instructor/pre-cohort-checklist.md), and TOOLS.md keeps a calendar of announced changes.
3. **Check age and region rules** for the cohort and pick a stack from [instructor/variants.md](../instructor/variants.md) before enrollment.
4. **New evidence** goes into the [research notes](notes/) with a link, then into this page if it changes a decision. Keep every claim linked to a source.
5. **New incidents** can be added to the [case studies](../resources/case-studies.md) only with sources, and conflicting reports should be flagged.
6. **Report problems** through the process in [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md), and run its checks before sending a pull request.
